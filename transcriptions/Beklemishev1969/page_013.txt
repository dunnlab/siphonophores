Colonies of Cristatella (Fig. 208, B, C) differ from colonies of the great majority of Bryozoa in their mobility: they crawl by means of the sole. As in other groups of animals, mobility of the colony at once produces a need for higher integration. A Cristatella colony is in fact a compact whole with a regular biradiate shape; complete fusion of the cystids leads to development of a locomotor musculature and a neural plexus common to the whole colony, making locomotion in response to external stimuli possible. A common colonial coelom ensures uniformity of the internal environment of the colony. The colony is able to reproduce as such by transverse fission; it has the capacity to restore its shape after injury; in brief, it is a real organic individual (P. Svetlov, 1935). As for the separate zooids, the cystids have completely lost their individuality and become part of the common body wall of the colony. The polypides differ in no way from those of other Phylactolaemata, each of them representing the front end of the body and the complex of internal organs of a relatively-perfect bilaterally-symmetrical animal.

Polymorphism appears in Phylactolaemata only in the existence of oecia, which here are modified polypides. They are formed from young buds that remain in the earliest stage of development, and after formation of an ovum they are transformed into simple epithelial sacs, from which the larva later emerges directly to the exterior.

All marine Bryozoa, i.e. Stenolaemata and Gymnolaemata, differ from Phylactolaemata in having polypides of much simpler structure, which denotes a definite step towards suppression of their individuality by that of colonies. On the other hand their cystids are generally not fused together, and in that respect the zooids are more independent than in most Phylactolaemata. The larvae of Stenolaemata and Gymnolaemata, on attachment, almost always produce a single zooid, whose method of development may be regarded as necrotic metamorphosis. At a certain stage of their life, therefore, marine Bryozoa are solitary sessile animals, and in that respect coloniality is less dominant in them than in Phylactolaemata.¹ The first individual (the founder of the colony) is called an oozooid or ancestrula. The latter name is due to the fact that in Gymnolaemata that individual often has some primitive features as compared with the other members of the colony—the blastozooids. Since the oozooid usually possesses no sexual products, the life cycle of Stenolaemata and Gymnolaemata must be regarded as an alternation of generations—to be precise, metagenesis.
