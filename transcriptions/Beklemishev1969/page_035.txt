When the pre-buds settle in medial rows they are arranged in groups. In each group one bud changes into a phorozooid and the others into its 'deputy' and into sexual pre-buds. The phorozooid grows into a Doliolum-like individual, on whose stalk a sexual pre-bud settles. Together with the latter the phorozooid breaks off and thenceforth lives independently, and the nearest 'deputy' takes its place. A mature colony thus consists of permanent members—oozooid and gastrozooids—and temporary members—phorozooids and sexual pre-buds. The colony is of 'monarchic' type, but with marked polymorphism and considerable independence of the subordinate zooids.

After breaking away the phorozooid swims freely in the sea, and the sexual pre-bud on it divides and forms several gonozooids, so that a small free-swimming colony is formed. On maturity the gonozooids in turn separate from their phorozooid and thenceforth lead the life of independent solitary sexual individuals.

Everything here is extraordinary—the remarkable polymorphism of the colony; its high integration, with relative independence of the separate individuals; the close physiological connection, without true organic unity. But the most surprising feature is the transportation of pre-buds by phorocytes, which deposit them in groups in strictly regular order. Here we have an unusually striking example of a phenomenon very common but too often overlooked—the role of the behaviour of cellular elements in the morphogenesis of a multicellular animal, and the problem of the stimuli determining the movement and final location of cells—the builders of an organism—is placed clearly before us.

8. CONCLUSIONS ON THE TYPES OF COLONIES OF METAZOA

Among coelenterates the only analogues of the linear colonies of worms are the strobilae of Anthozoa and Scyphozoa; in both cases the colony arises through paratomy; in both cases separating individuals reach sexual maturity sooner or later (Discomedusae, Syllidae). A difference is that the strobilae of worms (except cestodes) are free-moving; ecologically they have very little in common with the strobilae of Gonactinia or Discomedusae. Therefore there is no similarity in their external appearance. Among Protozoa the nearest analogue of linear colonies of worms is the chains of the parasitic infusorian Anoplophrya (Holotricha Astomata, Fig. 91, D).
